## [Libraries and the commons](http://cavlec.yarinareth.net/2009/01/02/libraries-and-the-commons/)

A couple-three things happened over the holidays that strike me as of a piece, and with implications for libraries that have digitization or digital-preservation programs of any sort at all (including institutional repositories).

One of them is that [Google turned off its sneakernet mass-data service](http://blog.wired.com/wiredscience/2008/12/googlescienceda.html), before really even turning it on. I have no inside intel on why this happened, but I do have two strong suspicions: one, uptake was minimal (which any card-carrying repo-rat could have told Google would happen, but oh well), and two, the sad few contributed datasets weren’t anything that would set the world on fire, especially given a critical lack of assessment or metadata or organization or anything *else* that would make the data worth keeping.

Another is [Yahoo firing the manager of Flickr Commons](http://george08.blogspot.com/2008/12/not-quite-what-i-had-in-mind.html). I’ve had [Tom Scheinfeldt’s reaction](http://www.foundhistory.org/2008/12/22/tragedy-at-the-commons/) pinned in Bloglines for a couple of weeks, because it’s typically smart and thoughtful; those interested should also read [the Wired story](http://blog.wired.com/business/2008/12/with-layoffs-wh.html) and especially the comments thereto.

Oh, and [JotSpot is disappearing](http://www.jot.com/). Grab your content quick.

However lefthandedly, these developments are somewhat encouraging to library efforts to develop in-house data curation and digitization programs; we can now legitimately say “one cannot trust the commercial market to develop or sustain these services.” I caution libraries against triumphalism, however. Go back and click the links in the above paragraphs. Do a page search on each for the string “librar”. Save for mention of the library participants in Flickr Commons, *the word barely appears*. Tom Scheinfeldt doesn’t even think about the Mason libraries (which makes me contemplate my own epic fail once again; I did earnestly *try* to make a connection with CHNM while I was there).

Note also Carl Malamud’s cluelessness in the Wired article about Flickr Commons as regards preservation of and access to high-quality versions of the images. Of *course* the Library of Congress is all over that—there is even a correction in the comments, though not by the Library of Congress itself. Clearly, however, the LoC hasn’t made their preservation role clear, nor is libraries’ reputation such that it goes without saying. *We are simply not seen* as a potential resource for sustainable digitization or dataset preservation.

Honestly, given the bumbling and fumbling I’ve seen in academic libraries over the last year or two with regard to data curation (the NSF’s DataNet trajectory is illustrative, though you have to be in the know to get the really good stories), I am hard-pressed to say that we *should* be the go-to people. I’m sure I don’t know who *else* is going to do it, but that’s not at all the same as saying we will.

It’s a pity. There’s an obvious need, and the business cases aren’t terribly hard to make, since after all there’s plenty of precedent, both of successful preservation programs and resources that disappeared for lack of them. One would think an institution desperately seeking to guard its indispensability would greet such an obvious need with glad cries… but I don’t see so much as a welcoming whimper.

So. Libraries. What are we going to do about this?

**Edited to add**: Carl Malamud pointed out to me in email that while the LoC is on top of things, the Smithsonian hasn’t always been, which is unquestionably true. I appreciate the clarification, and his good humor in its delivery. (It’s almost certain that Wired cut down whatever he actually said in any case, so it was bad of me to assume that any apparent cluelessness was genuine.)
