## [Not the future](http://cavlec.yarinareth.net/2009/03/17/not-the-future/)

A few weeks ago, I went to a perfectly ordinary, run-of-the-mill meeting about some local digital materials in need of a home. I go to such meetings regularly. It’s a normal part of what I do. Not groundbreaking, not strange or unusual in any way. Nor did I have to say anything particularly weird or techie, except in relation to inescapable characteristics of the materials at hand—the sort of thing most librarians will have thought about.

So I was rocked back on my mental heels to hear another librarian who had been at the meeting sigh and say, “What you do is the wave of the future.”

No. No, it’s not. It’s now. It’s happening now. It’s my daily reality *right now*.

I found myself applying my rusty lit-crit skills to that phrase, interrogating it, deconstructing it, and I’ve decided I don’t like it and I wish it would go away, at least with respect to digital scholarship and digital librarianship.

The thing about “the future” is that it’s not now. If it’s not now, those who are up to their eyeballs in now will ignore it—*do* ignore it. So saying that something is “the wave of the future” means that for many people, it’s file-and-forget. In my experience, there is no correlation whatever between this phenomenon and one’s y-axis position in an organization chart; I’ve seen it everywhere, in the rank-and-file and its leaders alike.

The future is also easy to sabotage, compared to the now. Now has inertia. Now has adherents. Now has employees. The future only has eye-gleams, which makes sabotage as simple as [blatant underresourcing](http://digital-scholarship.com/digitalkoans/2009/03/16/maverick-digital-project-manager-jobs/). It’s easy to play damaging rhetorical games with the future, too; I’ve seen too many smart people painted with the “uncritical tech-lover” brush not to know that.

So I’m with [Tom Scheinfeldt](http://www.foundhistory.org/2009/03/17/thinking-the-unthinkable/), who notices similar rhetorical chicanery in the digital humanities. Positioning ourselves as “the future” has thus far not gotten us where we need to go.

Instead, let’s point out that we are the now, and the problems we solve are likewise present-day. The data deluge, for example, isn’t a problem of “the future;” it’s a problem *today*. We can decide to solve it or not, to be sure, but that decision requires a completely different and in my opinion more productive cast of thought compared to “data is our future! we have to prepare now!”

(I worry sometimes about the data-curation programs at UNC and Illinois. I’m not worried about their student pool, or even about the quality of their instruction. I’m worried that they’re minting professionals who will not find jobs because “data is the future” instead of the now, and what good is that?)

I certainly mean to keep this in mind as I describe what I do for those who may not understand, and as I try to recruit buy-in. I am not the future. I am the now.
