1. Brainstorm with my staff for a theme, idea, snappy title, or some other method of pulling a bunch of stuff to highlight. This may take more or less time depending upon whether we've been asked to do an exhibit in conjunction with a conference or celebration, or whether we've just decided it's time to change out the stuff in the cases.
2. Once the theme has been found, figure out which one of the three of us is primarily responsible for putting together the exhibit. Although I'm the curator, we're a small staff, so I rarely do more than 2 exhibits per year; my amazing staff does the others, and since they enjoy the work and are good at it, I'm perfectly happy to share the love.
3. Pull a bunch of books (too many to fit in the cases) that fit the theme and might look cool.
4. Winnow down the best looking items for display.
5. Research the items and write blurbs for each item or each case describing what things are and why we picked them.
6. Put books in cases, add paper labels with blurbs, re-lock cases. Put up signs with the exhibit's title.
7. Ta da! \*jazz hands\* ~~World Domination.~~ Repeat as necessary, every three months or so.

*when you're already an expert.*

I'm

*not*a comics expert, by a long shot. I didn't grow up reading them or collecting them. I don't publish scholarly articles about them. I build our collection of them as carefully as I can, but I rely heavily gifts for that collection, so it's more a matter of "accepting things we don't own already and try to fill in gaps" than "making concerted efforts to purchase things that I know we need because I follow the field so very closely." I'm a generalist, responsible for 39 other collections. There's only so much of the collection development love and brain-space to go around.

Scratch the shiny veneer of "what I know about what we have," and you will see a deep chasm of "what I don't know about what we have," and "what I don't know that we should have but need to acquire." I was particularly nervous about the gallery talk exposing gaps in my (not-so) Vast Curatorial Knowledge (TM).

Thus, I have spent time in what I lovingly refer to as "fake it till you make it generalist curator school." It is (and has been) time-consuming, but there have been certain benefits.

1. Colleagues and friends, long-time comics readers with more expertise than I, have kindly reviewed my labels for accuracy, and have helped to influence my choices for this exhibit at my request. You have taught me much, and for your kindness in taking the time to do that, I thank you (you know who you are).
2. On the recommendation of those same kind colleagues and friends, *Comic Book Nation* helped me identify important movements, titles, storylines, and issues that should be covered. I've got a bunch of other scholarly books about comics to help me fill in gaps and get a feel for scholarly interpretations in the comics field that I will be skimming in the next couple of weeks. I am learning about the history of comics scholarship.
3. *I got to spend a couple of days doing nothing but reading comics and graphic novels from our collections*. This is called "research." How awesome is that?
4. As I have learned about the comics we hold in relation to putting together the exhibit, I have been able to identify gaps that need filling more accurately.

I still don't feel like an expert yet, but putting together this exhibit has ended up expanding my curatorial knowledge about our comics collections, which is always a good thing. I feel as though I've moved past the panic stage now, and have expanded the area of "what I know about what we have" a bit more than I had before, which will hopefully be enough for now.

It's been kind of fun, too. I've discovered a few newer titles that I hadn't heard about before, which I will be adding to the collections if we don't have them already, and got to read or reread some milestone titles to refresh my memory, and reinforce my notion that this is truly an American popular art form worth collecting and studying.

Not bad. Not bad at all.
