## [The Information Concierge](http://cavlec.yarinareth.net/2009/04/22/the-information-concierge/)

So I had a meeting this afternoon with some folks we didn’t know much about who might have some stuff we could archive. My job was to go in and find out what they had.

Any good reference librarian will tell you that the putative need you start with isn’t the real need. So I remembered all that stuff they taught me in library school about reference interviewing and listened to them talk about their program and their stuff and what they’re doing with it and where they’re getting stuck.

Archiving? Was the least of it. So, you know, I could have just stood up and said “sorry, not a whole lot I can do for you here” and walked away… but I’m a librarian; I am just not wired that way. What I ended up doing was telling them about half a dozen people or programs or initiatives on campus that I thought could help them with some part of their picture. I also offered to take care of one or two specific needs that are well within my area of expertise, and I promised to follow up back at the ranch to see what else we might be able to do.

Cross a reference librarian with a hotel concierge, and you have what I was during that meeting. And isn’t that a damn interesting model for a joint library/IT service?

We have the “I can’t find the services I need! I can’t find anyone else who knows how to do this particular thing I want to do!” conversation on MPOW’s campus on a regular basis. Constantly. Unendingly. IT and the library are particular loci of confusion, partly because like it or not, our services *do* increasingly overlap.

So imagine an office. Half of this office’s job is ferreting out who offers what services on campus. The other half of its job is connecting inquiries with appropriate services (and, when necessary, politely telling people that they are completely delusional). And the third half of its job is outreach—though I rather suspect that if this office is halfway competent in the other two halves of its job, word of mouth will spread like wildfire.

The office should have an incident tracker, just like a help desk, and every year or so it ought to be pumping out gap-analysis reports: what services should the institution be offering that it isn’t?

It’s hard for me to imagine a lose here. Problems get solved. People save time. Services find their audiences without costly and inefficient outreach. The institution learns about its capacities and has solid information on which to base resource-allocation decisions. The information concierge gets one hell of a fun and interesting job.

Why don’t we have these on sprawling campuses? I just bet they’d pay for themselves many times over.
