I’m writing this for #hackacad so I’m dialing back my flippant tone at the blog and looking at some issues more in depth than usual, OK?

Dealing with the gatekeeper archivist no longer is the only way to go for scholars and other researchers. The gatekeeper model had negative and positive aspects. If the subject matter expert archivist was unavailable when you did your research, you missed out on the chance to get the type of helpful personal attention many old school scholars have praised in book acknowledgements.  Yes, you could get a lot of information from the scope and content note and folder title list in a finding aid.  Doing detailed finding aids took up a lot of archival staff time although they often provided helpful context for researchers initially researching a topic. A recent discussion in an archivists’ forum pointed to one such archival [aid](http://www.nixonlibrary.gov/forresearchers/find/textual/central/subject/tr.php) which had an exhaustive amount of detail.  That model is becoming outmoded in some instances, and not just because individual records are being uploaded on the web.  Look for less context and less detail in some finding aids produced at institutions using the More Product Less Process approach (not all are) that archival experts have developed in recent years.  The new approach mostly affects conservation and arrangement but can affect description, too.

An institution such as the U.S. National Archives and Records Administration (NARA) holds an incredible amount of material. Over time, more and more of it will be available online. Alexander Stille observed of NARA in an [article](http://www.newyorker.com/archive/1999/03/08/1999_03_08_038_TNY_LIBRY_000017676) in the *New Yorker* in 1999 that “The Archives is currently the custodian of four billion pieces of paper; 9.4 million photographs; 338,029 films and videos; 2,648,918 maps and charts; and more than nine million aerial photographs.”

Since then, NARA and the Presidential Libraries that it administers have accessioned a great deal of additional material, much of it electronic. Ten years ago, the task of dealing with non-paper media seemed daunting. “The problem, in Charles Mayn’s view, is that nobody inside or outside government is making the tough decisions about what to store and what to throw away.” He also pointed to the role of the requester in some technical decisions. ‘We will keep those things that researchers happen to have requested and that consequently get copied onto new media,’ he says. The danger now is that such a vast accumulation of records makes it nearly impossible to distinguish the essential from the ephemeral.”

So how is that working out? Better than expected in some areas, worse in others. It isn’t that NARA decides what to keep based on *researcher* requests. Governmental appraisal principles still apply although NARA has found that electronic records management has proven very challenging for the federal agencies that create government records. However, requests sometimes do have an impact on what is digitized and put online. Keep that in mind. Depending on the time period they are studying and the volume of material, many researchers still have to come to NARA to study primary sources. But NARA is putting more and more material on the web. The new AOTUS, David Ferriero, is committed to this as part of the Open Government initiative.

Some of NARA’s archival processing is request-driven, some is systematic, and some is mandated. The Freedom of Information Act (FOIA) has had an enormous impact on what some NARA units process and when. Especially at the newer presidential libraries, much of what NARA posts online as digitized documents results from FOIA cases. As a scholar, you’re in the mix as a FOIA requestor along with ordinary citizens, journalists, muckrakers, and political advocacy groups. Requestors have various deadlines but generally within the government, FOIA operates on a first in, first out principle. However, as NARA’s head of presidential libraries [explained](http://news.nationaljournal.com/articles/071217nj1.htm), NARA has been able to modify that somewhat in some areas by establishing what it calls simple and complex queues:

“If you just had one long queue, if somebody had a request for 3 million pages, and you had a request for five pages, and you were behind the 3-million-page requester, it would be a long time before you would get your request. So, by having different queues, and the \[Archives\] staff rotating across the queues when they picked the next case to process, they are able to answer more of these simple requests and can turn some of them around fairly quickly.

We have several different queues at the \[Clinton\] library. There’s a queue for classified records. There’s a queue for audio-visual records. There’s a complex queue where there’s more than 10,000 pages.”

Even so, special occurrences can slow down researcher queues by affecting archivists’ customary assignments for a while. The need to make records available for a forthcoming confirmation hearing naturally results in an all-hands-on-deck effort to locate and process responsive documents.

Sometimes a researcher may try to affect who gets his materials processed first and who must wait behind him. Never discount the possibility that someone will yell and stamp his feet and threaten to go to his representative in order to “get his stuff—now!” Or that a special interest group will pressure NARA to allocate more, more, more resources to archival projects it wants completed quickly. Or that a blogger will whine and complain and make life miserable for archivists. Counterproductive? Sure. NARA tries to resist pressure but it isn’t always easy. That’s not to say there aren’t plenty of good-guy researchers who establish good relations with the archives and vice versa.

All in all, do-it-yourself-online-research has many advantages for scholars. They aren’t limited by their ability to travel to different geographic locations or by the hours an archival institution is open. (Facing budget constraints, NARA tried to cut back on research room hours a few years ago, as many other repositories have had to do. It eventually restored them. However, limited hours and shutdowns increasingly threaten more and more libraries and archival institutions.) The research process is becoming more and more depersonalized. Dealing with the gatekeeper archivist no longer is the only path to reaching records.

As NARA puts more out there, online, think through the implications. Prepare to bring more of yourself to the table. You won’t be sitting in a research room with helpful archivists on call to explain how the organization you’re studying was set up functionally and who the principals were and what were their relationships to each other. And where related documents might reside. You might have known some of that already but not always, depending on how new a subject was to you. And there may not be detailed finding aids. But you’ll have much greater ability to browse and pull up documents, without putting in call slips, hoping no one else is using the box you want, and waiting for the next pull time. The future increasingly is DIY research, you can count on that.
