# Online CVs

0After the first day of THATCamp, I had a great conversation with Katherine Fitzpatrick about scholarly/academic standing and publications. She's doing a wonderful experiment in open contributions and reviews to her own work, one that's inspired me to start doing some work on the Drupal tech side to see if I can support that.

0But the really important thing was that it made a nice parallel to the idea of using Omeka to build an online CV. The key thing in both cases is trying to rethink the presentation of one's scholarly self. She's working on -- and implementing to see what happens -- an online presentation of her scholarship. I'm working on the same thing in a CV.

0As a starter, you can't avoid this from Dan Cohen:

0 Bingo. @kfitz: "We're moving from a pre-publishing review process to a post-publishing filtering process." #thatcamp


0[link](http://twitter.com/dancohen/statuses/14512738374)

0This was retweeted at least nine times. That in itself is a measure of influence and significance. I won't claim to go so far as to say that it is as influential as a monograph. The rapidity with which it was retweeted, though, does speak to the influence -- and resonance -- that the statement had. I'll also grant that the retweeters are roughly of like mind concerning the state of higher ed. and the review process therein. But I think there's really something there.

0When it comes to one's CV, I think that the point is especially important. She points to a notion that there might be better metrics for evaluating a potential hire than the list of publications. Instead, one's place in the academy might -- just might -- be fairly measured by finding a metric for where others in your discipline put you in the rank and filter of what they want coming to you.

0In this case, we have a neat example of retweets signaling that they want their twitter networks to pay attention to this tweet. It spoke to the retweeters, and they hope that it will speak to others in their networks. That's scholarly significance that exists outside the metrics of traditional publishing.

0What does this suggest for an online CV? First, it has to go all-out with online-ness. A web page that closely mimics the print version doesn't quite cut it. Instead, let's use the metrics of the web. What have you said or presented that others tweet about? What posts have you written that get a lot of traffic? What "approved" online publications like to things you have published online?

0I'm just starting to think about this, but here's the two places I'm jumping off from. First, my online CV has a link called "Google Me". We ought to assume that anytime we submit an application for anything, our names will get plugged into Google. So let's acknowledge that, and say we acknowledge that, by giving that link? Google, I hear, has been pretty successful in presenting relevant information to the majority of people. What if that actually works for higher ed, too? What if good rankings in search engines really do offer an insight into one's place in academia. Not the only insight -- but maybe one to consider alongside publications, conference talks, etc.?

0Second, I've got a similar link to recent mentions on Twitter. That one is definitely riskier. On a good day, there will be wonderful retweets and mentions from a wide variety of sources. If they retweet something from me, that implicitly says that I have something to offer to one of the communities they are part of. That's important. And it's worth the risk of whatever happens when I have a so-so to bad day.

0The bigger picture of how to use the metrics available online is still mostly uncharted territory, I think. If I have a publication online, how do I expose the Google Analytics for it that I'm watching and interested in? To what degree do or should that data influence further research and project development? To what degree is the academy ready to count data from Google Analytics in tenure and promotion considerations?

0The only key point that I want to aim for is that "online CV" should not mean recreating the print version of your CV in a web accessible way. Rather, "online CV" calls up a myriad of reconsiderations about authority, methodology of evaluation, theories of (academic/scholarly) self, and more. We all need to face these head-on.
